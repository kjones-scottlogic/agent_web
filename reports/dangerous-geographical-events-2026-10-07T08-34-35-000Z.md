# COMPREHENSIVE REPORT: DANGEROUS GEOGRAPHICAL EVENTS

## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This report synthesizes research on major geographical hazards affecting global populations. Over the past two decades, extreme events have intensified, driven primarily by climate change and human land-use modifications. Key findings show that 1.6 billion people were impacted by natural disasters between 2000-2019, with economic losses averaging $40 billion annually in flooding alone and tropical cyclones accounting for nearly half of all disaster losses (1980-2018).

## I. SEISMIC EVENTS AND EARTHQUAKES

### Causes and Mechanisms

Earthquakes are primarily caused by movement of tectonic plates within Earth's crust, with stress accumulating at fault lines where plates meet (https://www.usgs.gov/). The Earth's surface comprises large tectonic plates thousands of kilometers in horizontal extent and approximately 100 km thick, moving at about the rate fingernails grow (https://www.britannica.com/science/plate-tectonics/Seismic-tomography/). Elastic strain accumulates at plate boundaries until stress overcomes friction at faults, causing them to slip and release stored strain energy as seismic waves (https://www.bgs.ac.uk/discovering-geology/earth-hazards/earthquakes/what-causes-earthquakes/).

Seismic waves occur when energy trapped within the Earth's crust escapes, usually caused by pieces of Earth's crust bumping into or sliding under one another (https://www.mtu.edu/geo/community/seismology/learn/earthquake-cause/).

### Detection and Measurement

Seismic waves are detected with a seismometer containing a suspended pendulum-like mass, connected to a seismograph which records motion as a seismogram (https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/seismometers-seismographs-seismograms-whats-difference-how-do-they-work). To record ground motion in all three dimensions, seismologists use three separate sensors: Z component (up/down), E component (east/west), and N component (north/south) (https://www.bgs.ac.uk/discovering-geology/earth-hazards/earthquakes/how-are-earthquakes-detected/). Earthquake location, depth, and strength can be calculated from seismograph data using the time interval between different wave arrivals (https://www.cliffsnotes.com/study-guides/geology/earthquakes/monitoring-earthquakes).

The Richter scale is generally used to measure the size or magnitude of earthquakes and other seismic events (https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/seismometers-seismographs-seismograms-whats-difference-how-do-they-work).

### Historical Major Events

The 1960 Chile earthquake (M9.5) is the strongest earthquake in recorded history, followed by the 1964 Alaska earthquake (M9.2) and 2004 Sumatra earthquake (M9.1) (https://earthquaketoday.org/earthquakes/biggest). The Turkey-Syria earthquake of February 6, 2023 (M7.8 and M7.5 doublet) is the deadliest event of the 21st century so far (https://www.volcanodiscovery.com/earthquakes/major.html).

### Technological Advances

Machine learning applications have advanced earthquake seismology fields including earthquake catalog development, seismicity analysis, ground motion prediction, and geodetic data applications (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40623-024-01982-0). A new computational approach speeds up complex seismic simulations by approximately 1,000 times, making risk assessments far more practical (https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/01/260106001918.htm).

## II. VOLCANIC ERUPTIONS

### Formation and Mechanisms

Volcanoes form in three main settings: subduction zones where oceanic plates converge with continental plates, rift zones where plates move apart, and hotspots where plumes of hot mantle material rise toward the surface (https://sandbox.dss.ucdavis.edu/how-do-volcanoes-form-and-erupt). Temperature and pressure increase dramatically with depth, reaching up to 3000°C, which causes rock to melt and form magma (https://www.orchidsinternationalschool.com/physics/volcano-eruption).

Volcanic eruptions occur when magma below the surface rises due to being less dense than surrounding rock, causing gas bubbles to form and pressure to increase until the magma bursts out as lava, ash, and rocks (https://home.nps.gov/subjects/volcanoes/eruption-classifications.htm).

### Eruption Types

Eruptions can be categorized as effusive (involving low-viscosity basaltic magma with low gas content) or explosive (involving more viscous magma with higher gas content) (https://geology.com/volcanoes/types-of-volcanic-eruptions). The six main eruption styles in order from least to most explosive are: Hawaiian, Strombolian, Vulcanian, Peléean, Plinian, and Ultraplinian types (https://britannica.com/science/volcano/Six-types-of-eruptions).

Three fundamental categories of eruptions are magmatic (gas decompression within magma), phreatic (superheating of steam), and phreatomagmatic (direct interaction of magma and water) (https://geology.com/volcanoes/types-of-volcanic-eruptions).

### Lava Flow Types

The four main types of lava flows are pāhoehoe (with ropy, smooth surfaces), 'ā'ā (rough and jagged with clinkery texture), block lava (intermediate composition with large angular blocks), and pillow lava (which makes up 70% of Earth's surface) (https://home.nps.gov/articles/000/lava-flow-forms.htm). Lava viscosity is primarily controlled by silica content, gas content, and temperature, which determines how lava behaves - high viscosity lava flows slowly and traps gases, while low viscosity lava flows easily and releases gases (https://volcanodb.com/types-of-lava).

### Historical Catastrophic Events

Mount Vesuvius erupted in AD 79 near Naples, Italy, destroying Pompeii and killing an estimated 16,000 people over two days of continuous eruption (https://activewild.com/famous-volcanoes/). Krakatoa erupted in August 1883, with four tremendous explosions on August 27th, the third of which could be heard 4,800 km away and is considered the loudest sound in history; the eruption killed over 36,000 people through tsunamis (https://activewild.com/famous-volcanoes/).

Mount St. Helens erupted on May 18, 1980, in Washington State with a massive lateral blast that killed 57 people and flattened 600 square kilometers of forest, making it the deadliest and most economically destructive volcanic event in US history (https://activewild.com/famous-volcanoes/).

### Global Impact Statistics

Between 1900 and 2008, there were 91,789 deaths, 14,068 injuries, and 4.72 million people affected by volcanic events globally (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3644290/). More than 500 million people live within the potential exposure range of a volcano (https://environmental-geol.pressbooks.tru.ca/chapter/effects-of-volcanic-eruptions-on-humans-and-on-earth-systems/).

### Mortality and Effects

Primary causes of mortality in recent volcanic eruptions are ash asphyxiation, thermal injuries from pyroclastic flow, and trauma (https://environmental-geol.pressbooks.tru.ca/chapter/effects-of-volcanic-eruptions-on-humans-and-on-earth-systems/). Volcanic eruptions cause direct effects including loss of life and property, suffocation and respiratory hazards from ash, destruction of agricultural fields and livestock, and elimination of natural habitats and ecosystems (https://senecalearning.com/en-GB/revision-notes/igcse/geography/cambridge-international/4-3-2-primary-effects-of-volcanoes).

Volcanic emissions can lead to acid rain, which harms aquatic life and damages vegetation, but soil around active volcanoes tends to be fertile, allowing for abundant food production (https://environmental-geol.pressbooks.tru.ca/chapter/effects-of-volcanic-eruptions-on-humans-and-on-earth-systems/).

### Current Monitoring

Great Sitkin Volcano in Alaska is at WATCH alert level with a slow eruption continuing within the summit crater since July 2021, monitored using seismic sensors, satellite data, webcams, and infrasound networks (https://avo.alaska.edu/news/hans/DOI-USGS-AVO-2026-08-24T19:39:25+00:00). Mount Kupreanof in Alaska is at ADVISORY alert level with low-level seismic activity and steam and SO2 emissions (https://avo.alaska.edu/news/hans/DOI-USGS-AVO-2026-08-31T18:53:21+00:00).

## III. TSUNAMIS

### Definition and Characteristics

A tsunami is a series of ocean waves generated by sudden displacements in the sea floor, landslides, or volcanic activity, while a storm surge is an abnormal rise of water generated by a storm over and above predicted astronomical tides (https://rdsic.edu.vn/blog/blog-6/1259029from-the-expression-on-record-in-line-22-it-can-be-inferred-that-the-tsunami-that-accompanied-the-kr-vi-cb.html).

Tsunamis involve the entire water column from the ocean floor to the surface, can travel across entire ocean basins at speeds close to 700 kilometers per hour, and can last for several hours, while storm surges are relatively short-lived events lasting only a few hours (https://geo.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Oceanography/Oceanography_101_(Miracosta)/10%3A_Waves/10.13%3A_Tsunamis).

### Causation

The vast majority of tsunamis are triggered by underwater earthquakes at subduction zones where an oceanic plate is forced beneath a continental plate, causing vertical displacement of the seafloor that displaces massive volumes of water (https://www.tutorchase.com/notes/edexcel-a-level/geography/1-3-3-causes-and-mechanisms-of-tsunamis). Tsunami-generating earthquakes typically occur at convergent boundaries with reverse or thrust faulting and often exceed magnitude 7.0 on the Moment Magnitude Scale, with roughly 80% of all historical tsunamis caused by underwater earthquakes (https://anantamias.com/tsunami-causes-and-formation/?pdf=1).

Secondary causes of tsunamis include submarine landslides, volcanic eruptions, and meteorite impacts, which can generate extraordinarily powerful tsunamis that are often localized (https://upscsociology.in/q-what-are-tsunamis-how-and-where-are-they-formed-what-are-their-consequences-explain-with-examples/).

### Catastrophic Events

The December 26, 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami was triggered by a magnitude 9.1 earthquake off Sumatra island, reaching as high as 167 feet (51 meters) and causing flooding up to three miles inland, killing approximately 230,000 people in a dozen countries (https://www.ktvu.com/news/historical-tsunamis-that-have-struck-around-the-world). The 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami displaced 1.7 million people and caused roughly $13 billion in economic losses, with impacts observed in 17 countries across Southeastern Asia, Southern Asia, and Africa (https://www.preventionweb.net/quick/90674).

The 2011 Japan tsunami, originating from a 9.1 magnitude earthquake off Honshu Island, resulted in 18,453 deaths (https://www.worldatlas.com/articles/deadliest-tsunamis-since-1900.html).

### Early Warning Systems

The Pacific Tsunami Warning Center (PTWC) was established in 1949 to monitor the Pacific Ocean for tsunami activity and provide early warnings (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4608033/). Coastal communities should receive three standardized, accurate, real-time tsunami warning products: tsunami energy estimate, flooding maps, and tsunami-induced harbour current maps to minimize impacts and guide evacuations (https://www.preventionweb.net/publications/view/19689).

Japan's tsunami preparedness system combines instantaneous seismic monitoring with public outreach, clearly marked evacuation routes, and regular drills, with students in K-12 schools routinely practicing evacuation procedures (https://ncdp.columbia.edu/?p=101831). By 2024, Indonesia planned to have 533 seismograph sensors in place with more reliable, timely, and accurate warning technology following deficiencies revealed by the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami (https://theaseanmagazine.asean.org/?p=6131).

### Economic Impact

An estimated 750,000 people would face evacuation and boats in marinas would be damaged or lost in the event of a major tsunami affecting California's coast (https://www.foxnews.com/us/report-details-potential-tsunami-disruptions-to-californias-economy-and-coastal-communities.amp). The twin ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach could lose $1.2 billion in business if shuttered for at least two days due to strong tsunami currents, with broader economic losses to the region potentially topping $700 million (https://www.smdp.com/report-tsunami-would-swamp-californias-economy.md).

Because 100% of Long Beach's developed land, population, and businesses are located within the tsunami hazard zone, losses caused by a tsunami could be catastrophic (https://www.cbsnews.com/losangeles/news/report-balboa-island-long-beach-businesses-at-risk-in-tsunami-event).

### Modeling and Forecasting

The objective of tsunami modeling research is to develop numerical models for faster and more reliable forecasts of tsunamis propagating through the ocean and striking coastal communities (https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/modeling-and-visualization-of-tsunamis/). The MOST (Method of Splitting Tsunami) model, developed by Titov of PMEL and Synolakis of University of Southern California, simulates three processes of tsunami evolution: earthquake, transoceanic propagation, and inundation of dry land (https://doaj.org/article/44b13ef540db4d9987b39745e43c1963).

### Preparedness and Resilience

Emergency preparedness for tsunami includes emergency planning, capability development, community engagement, and running exercises to practice plans; tsunami risk can be reduced through a range of risk reduction activities and good planning (https://knowledge.aidr.org.au/media/12000/aidr_tsunami-planning_qg4.pdf). A tsunami evacuation or escape building (TEB) is a temporary shelter for evacuation during a tsunami and must be located close to population centers and accessible via roads and transportation systems (https://knowledge.aidr.org.au/media/11998/aidr_tsunami-planning_qg6.pdf).

A tsunami resilient community is prepared for and properly responds to tsunamis to minimize loss of life and disruption to normal activities, requiring key technical elements including evacuation plans based on tsunami hazard maps and accurate real-time forecasts (https://www.weather.gov/media/tsunamiready/resources/TRTierTwo.pdf).

## IV. STORM SURGE

### Definition and Causes

Storm surge is defined as an abnormal rise of sea level above predicted astronomical tide, driven by storm winds pushing water toward the coast and the storm's low atmospheric pressure and wave setup (https://toolkit.climate.gov/storm-surge). Storm surge is caused by a combination of powerful hurricane winds pushing seawater toward the coast and low atmospheric pressure at the storm's center, with the faster the wind speed, the more water piles up (https://www.sofarocean.com/posts/what-is-a-storm-surge-and-what-causes-it).

Storm surge is the number one killer in hurricanes, often causing more damage than the winds themselves (https://www.encyclopedia.com/earth-and-environment/atmosphere-and-weather/weather-and-climate-terms-and-concepts/storm-surge).

### Vulnerability and Impacts

6.5 million households in the United States are located in areas prone to storm surge, making assessment of vulnerability of structures and residential communities to coastal flooding an important concern (https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/built-environment/articles/10.3389/fbuil.2018.00004/epub). Tampa/St. Petersburg is the metropolitan area most vulnerable to storm surge flooding damage with a loss potential of US$175 billion due to unique coastline features, local bathymetry, and low coastal elevations (https://www.canadianunderwriter.ca/news/claims/tampa-st-petersburg-most-vulnerable-to-storm-surge-flooding-damage-with-loss-potential-of-us175-billion-karen-clark/).

Hurricane Sandy (2012) damaged or destroyed over 650,000 structures, and when storm surge arrives simultaneously with high tide it can raise water levels 20 feet or more above mean sea level (https://gravitee.io/corpus/gen-174/typhoon-tracy/storm-surge.html).

## V. TROPICAL CYCLONES (HURRICANES/TYPHOONS/CYCLONES)

### Definition and Regional Terms

Typhoon, hurricane, and tropical cyclone all refer to the same meteorological phenomenon - a tropical rotating storm system with winds of 64 knots or more (118 km/h). The terms are used differently based on geographic region (https://www.boatnews.com/story/29580/hurricane-cyclone-and-typhoon-whats-the-difference). Hurricanes occur in the North Atlantic and northeast Pacific Ocean; typhoons occur in the Western Pacific Ocean; and cyclones occur in the western South Pacific Ocean and Indian Ocean (https://www.accuweather.com/en/hurricane/hurricanes-vs-typhoons-vs-cyclones-what-is-the-difference/1812211/amp).

### Formation Requirements

Hurricanes form when warm air near the surface rises and cools, with water vapor condensing and producing tremendous heat. This process requires warm sea surface temperatures of at least 26.5 degrees Celsius (about 80 Fahrenheit) (https://livescience.com/planet-earth/hurricanes/birth-of-a-hurricane-what-meteorologists-look-for-as-they-hunt-for-early-signs-of-a-tropical-cyclone-forming).

Tropical cyclones usually form at least 5 degrees from the equator because the Coriolis force, crucial for the initial spin-up of the cyclonic system, is weaker near the equator (https://www.tropicalweather.net/what-causes-hurricanes.html). Hurricanes require atmospheric instability with warm surface air that rises and remains warmer than surrounding air, creating thunderstorms, and they need plenty of moisture as dry air can weaken the storm (https://www.tropicalweather.net/how-do-hurricanes-form.html).

Low vertical wind shear is essential for hurricane formation, as strong vertical wind shear can tear a developing hurricane apart (https://www.theweathernetwork.com/en/news/science/explainers/birth-of-a-hurricane-what-meteorologists-look-for-as-they-hunt-for-early-signs-of-a-tropical-cyclone-formating).

### Classification System

The Saffir-Simpson hurricane scale was developed in 1971 by civil engineer Herbert Saffir and meteorologist Robert Simpson to classify hurricane intensity based on maximum surface wind speed (https://www.farmersalmanac.com/saffir-simpson-scale). The Saffir-Simpson scale has five categories: Category 1 (74-95 mph), Category 2 (96-110 mph), Category 3 (111-129 mph), Category 4 (130-156 mph), and Category 5 (157 mph or higher) (https://www.skybrary.aero/articles/saffir-simpson-hurricane-wind-scale-sshws). The National Hurricane Center classifies hurricanes of Category 3 and above as major hurricanes (https://glossary.ametsoc.org/wiki/Saffir-simpson_hurricane_scale).

### Global Distribution and Seasonality

72% of tropical storms form in the Northern Hemisphere, while 28% develop in the Southern Hemisphere, typically forming between 5 degrees and 30 degrees north and south of the equator (https://wmo.int/resources/wmo-fact-sheets/tropical-cyclone-climatology). Most tropical cyclone systems originate in the tropical Atlantic, the Eastern and Western Pacific, and the Indian Ocean, with specific notable regions including the Caribbean Sea, Gulf of Mexico, Southeast Asia, and the Bay of Bengal (https://community.wmo.int/node/37660).

Northern hemisphere cyclones are most active from June to November, peaking when sea temperatures are highest, while Southern hemisphere cyclones are most active from November to April (https://tutorchase.com/notes/cie-a-level/geography/9-3-1-distribution-of-atmospheric-hazards). Peak cyclone activity typically occurs in late summer and early fall, driven by seasonal migration of the Intertropical Convergence Zone (https://www.meted.ucar.edu/hurrican/strike/text/htc_t2.htm).

### Historical Deadliest Events

The Bhola cyclone in East Pakistan (November 12, 1970) is the deadliest tropical cyclone on record with approximately 500,000+ fatalities (https://wunderground.com/hurricane/articles/deadliest-tropical-cyclones). Seven of the 10 deadliest hurricanes, typhoons, and cyclones in recorded history occurred in the Bay of Bengal region, including the Bhola cyclone (1970, 150,000-550,000 deaths), Hooghly River cyclone (1737, 350,000 deaths), and Haiphong typhoon (1881, 300,000 deaths) (https://www.aljazeera.com/amp/news/2017/5/18/deadliest-storms-of-all-time-identified-by-wmo).

Super Typhoon Nina in China (1975) killed 171,000 people, and Cyclone Nargis in Myanmar (2008) resulted in 138,366 deaths (https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna24488385).

### Economic Impact

Hurricane Katrina and Hurricane Harvey are the costliest tropical cyclones on record, each causing $125 billion in damages (https://wunderground.com/hurricane/articles/deadliest-tropical-cyclones). From 1980 to 2018, tropical cyclones were responsible for nearly half of all natural disaster losses worldwide, with damage totaling USD 2,111 billion (https://www.savemyexams.com/gcse/geography/edexcel/a/18/revision-notes/2-weather-hazards-and-climate-change/2-2-tropical-cyclones/2-2-3-impacts-and-responses-to-tropical-cyclones).

The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration estimates an average cost of $22.8 billion per hurricane (https://implan.com/resources/blog/hurricane-season-2024/).

### Climate Change Impacts

Warming of the surface ocean from anthropogenic climate change is likely fueling more powerful tropical cyclones, with ocean temperature most strongly correlating to the rate of cyclone intensification (https://www.downtoearth.org.in/environment/increased-typhoon-intensity-linked-to-ocean-warming-50042). The strong rise in typhoon peak intensity over the past 35 years (about five meters per second, equivalent to half a category in typhoon strength) can be mostly attributed to unusual local upper-ocean warming rates (https://theconversation.com/increased-typhoon-intensity-linked-to-ocean-warming-42536).

If the world warmed by two degrees Celsius by 2100, there could be a five percent increase in maximum cyclonic wind speeds, and the proportion of severe tropical cyclones (category 4 & 5) is projected to increase (https://www.gfdl.noaa.gov/research_highlight/climate-change-is-probably-increasing-the-intensity-of-tropical-cyclones/). Rising sea level amplifies the destructive power of individual tropical cyclones through flooding, with a substantial contribution from anthropogenic climate change at the global scale (https://climate.gov/news-features/understanding-climate/climate-change-probably-increasing-intensity-tropical-cyclones).

Tropical cyclone precipitation rates are projected to increase due to enhanced atmospheric moisture associated with anthropogenic global warming (https://www.downtoearth.org.in/news/climate-change/intensity-of-tropical-cyclones-to-increase-next-century-due-to-climate-change-study-76253).

### Preparedness

An emergency preparedness kit should sustain individuals and families for at least 72 hours and include non-perishable food, bottled water (1 gallon per person per day), flashlights, batteries, first-aid supplies, medications, and important documents in waterproof pouches (https://hseblog.com/hurricane-safety). Permanent storm shutters provide the best protection for windows, with an alternative being to board up windows with 5/8 inch marine plywood cut to fit (https://www.farmersalmanac.com/hurricane-safety-tips).

Individuals should identify at least two evacuation routes in advance and know where they will go, how they will get there, and where they will stay if evacuation is ordered (https://www.wafb.com/2025/05/30/safety-tips-prepare-you-hurricane-season/). It is recommended to withdraw extra cash (approximately $200 to $500 in small bills) and fill the car's gas tank before a hurricane, as ATMs, credit card terminals, and gas stations may be unavailable during and after storms (https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/blog/2012/08/28/hurricane-safety-tips).

### Social and Economic Impacts

Economic growth and over-development have the strongest correlation with trends in increased damages from tropical cyclones, with time-delayed effects such as supply-chain interruptions only visible after certain time lags (https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/forecasting-the-rising-economical-impact-of-tropical-storms/).

Tropical cyclones cause loss of life, displacement of communities, and widespread damage to homes, businesses, and critical infrastructure, with long-term social and economic consequences including disruptions to livelihoods, food and water security, and healthcare systems (https://www.classace.io/answers/discuss-your-own-views-on-the-impact-of-tropical-cyclones).

Recovery from severe cyclones involves addressing damage to essential infrastructure and social sectors, coastal erosion from storm surges, and disruption of access to basic needs. Recovery can be slow due to budgetary and human resource constraints (https://recovery.preventionweb.net/es/node/67492). The total economic value of damage and losses from Cyclone Gita in 2018 was estimated at about 37.8 percent of Tonga's nominal gross domestic product, illustrating the profound economic impact on developing nations (https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10640-021-00541-5).

## VI. TORNADOES AND SEVERE THUNDERSTORMS

### Definitions

A tornado is defined as a rapidly rotating column of air extending vertically from the surface to the base of a cumuliform cloud, with a diameter typically of 2 km or less and lasting 100-1000 seconds (https://glossary.ametsoc.org/wiki/Tornado). Tornado wind speeds at the EF0 threshold are at least 65 mph (29 m/s), with wind velocity differences across the circulation exceeding 40 m/s within 200 m of the surface (https://glossary.ametsoc.org/wiki/Tornado).

A severe thunderstorm is defined by meeting at least one of three criteria: straight-line wind gusts of 58 mph (50 knots) or higher, hail of 1-inch (quarter size) diameter or larger, or a tornado (https://www.weather.gov/spotterguide/glossary_m). About 100,000 thunderstorms rumble across the United States each year, but just 10% intensify into a severe thunderstorm (https://www.weather.gov/spotterguide/glossary_m).

### Formation Mechanisms

Wind shear, which refers to changes in wind speed or direction with height, is critical in tornado formation by creating horizontally spinning air that is tilted into vertical rotation (https://earthsky.org/?p=262712). Supercells are characterized by a deep, persistent rotating updraft called a mesocyclone, and are especially likely to spawn tornadoes when lower altitudes are particularly humid with strong wind shear (https://earthsky.org/?p=262712).

Tornadoes develop when strong upward suction from the rotating updraft and the cold pool from evaporating rain causes horizontally spinning air to be tilted vertical and contracted inward, spinning faster (https://earthsky.org/?p=262712).

### Geographic Shift and Seasonality

There has been a geographic shift of the densest tornado activity away from Tornado Alley in the Great Plains toward the Great Lakes and Southeast regions of the US (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.16975). Spring is the dominant season for tornadoes, representing over half of all tornado activity, while in summer tornadoes shift strongly into the northern half of the United States north of 40°N (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.16975).

The Southeastern United States has a bimodal annual distribution of tornado likelihood, meaning tornadoes peak twice a year in this region (https://scholarworks.montana.edu/handle/1/14849). In Tornado Alley, tornado likelihood has shifted toward earlier dates across decades, and in the Southeast, the date of peak tornado occurrence has shifted toward earlier dates by more than a week (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.16975).

Tornadoes are clustering on fewer days in the year, with days containing many tornadoes becoming more common (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.16975).

### Warning Systems

A tornado watch means weather conditions are favorable for severe thunderstorms and tornadoes, while a tornado warning means a tornado has been sighted or is indicated by radar (https://www.osha.gov/tornado/preparedness). The best shelter options for tornadoes include underground areas such as basements or storm cellars, or interior rooms on the lowest floor with reinforced concrete, brick or block construction and no windows (https://www.osha.gov/tornado/preparedness).

Vehicles, trailers, and mobile homes do not provide adequate protection from tornadoes (https://www.osha.gov/tornado/preparedness). If caught outside during a tornado with no shelter available, one should lie flat in a ditch or low-lying area away from the home, covering the head with hands (https://www.osha.gov/tornado/preparedness).

### Intensity Measurement

The Enhanced Fujita (EF) Scale rates tornado intensity based on damage severity with six categories: EF0 (65-85 mph), EF1 (86-110 mph), EF2 (111-135 mph), EF3 (136-165 mph), EF4 (166-200 mph), and EF5 (>200 mph) (https://glossary.ametsoc.org/wiki/enhanced-fujita-scale/). The Enhanced Fujita Scale was publicly unveiled on October 4, 2004, developed from 2000-2004 by the Fujita Scale Enhancement Project at Texas Tech University's Wind Science and Engineering Research Center (https://glossary.ametsoc.org/wiki/enhanced-fujita-scale/).

The U.S. National Weather Service adopted the EF Scale in 2007, replacing the original F scale developed by Dr. Tetsuya Theodore Fujita in 1971 (https://glossary.ametsoc.org/wiki/enhanced-fujita-scale/). The Enhanced Fujita Scale incorporates 28 damage indicators with 8 degrees of damage (DOD) for each, addressing limitations of the original F scale which lacked damage indicators and definitive wind speed correlations (https://glossary.ametsoc.org/wiki/enhanced-fujita-scale/).

### Historical Deadliest Tornadoes

The Tri-State Tornado of March 18, 1925, tore a 219-mile-long path across Missouri, Illinois and Indiana, claiming 695 lives with winds over 300 mph and traveling at 73 mph (https://www.worldatlas.com/articles/a-history-of-destruction-the-10-deadliest-tornadoes-in-the-us.html). The deadliest tornado in world history struck Bangladesh on April 26, 1989, killing an estimated 1,300 people and injuring 12,000 others (https://www.lovemoney.com/gallerylist/137368/deadly-and-devastating-tornadoes-throughout-the-worlds-history).

A 16th-century tornado in Malta (September 1551 or 1556) started as a waterspout at sea and killed an estimated 600 people, mostly aboard an armada of ships in the harbor (https://www.lovemoney.com/gallerylist/137368/deadly-and-devastating-tornadoes-throughout-the-worlds-history).

The Natchez Tornado on May 6, 1840, killed 317 people in southwestern Mississippi, making it one of the deadliest U.S. tornadoes on record (https://www.lovemoney.com/gallerylist/137368/deadly-and-devastating-tornadoes-throughout-the-worlds-history). The St. Louis tornado on May 27, 1896, killed 255 people and injured 1,000, making it the third-deadliest tornado in U.S. history (https://www.lovemoney.com/gallerylist/137368/deadly-and-devastating-tornadoes-throughout-the-worlds-history).

The 2011 Joplin, Missouri tornado claimed 161 lives, marking the first tornado in U.S. history to claim over 100 lives since 1953 (https://www.lovemoney.com/gallerylist/137368/deadly-and-devastating-tornadoes-throughout-the-worlds-history).

### Recent Trends and Forecasts

Scientists know warm weather is a key ingredient in tornadoes and that climate change is altering the environment in which these storms form, but cannot directly connect these factors due to ongoing research limitations (https://www.kunc.org/npr-news/2021-12-13/the-exact-link-between-tornadoes-and-climate-change-is-hard-to-draw-heres-why). As of 2024, the United States had 639 confirmed tornadoes, though preliminary counts indicated 1,855 tornadoes in 2024 (https://corporate.accuweather.com/?p=7778).

AccuWeather long-range experts predict 1,300-1,450 tornadoes to occur across the U.S. in 2025, which is less than the preliminary 2024 count of 1,855 tornadoes (https://corporate.accuweather.com/?p=7778).

### Climate Change Effects on Severe Weather

Certain conditions favorable to thunderstorms and tornadoes are occurring more often as climate warms, with severe weather expanding into historically less-active seasons and regions (https://squirrel-preview.climatecentral.org/toolkit-severe-weather). Thunderstorm wind damage is happening more often as the climate warms, with the central U.S. experiencing a fivefold increase in the geographic area affected by damaging straight-line winds in the past 40 years (https://squirrel-preview.climatecentral.org/toolkit-severe-weather).

Tornado Alley has shifted eastward since 1979, with increased tornadic activity observed in the South, Southeast, and Ohio Valley (https://greenly.earth/en-us/blog/ecology-news/does-climate-change-have-a-real-impact-on-tornadoes).

## VII. FLOODING AND FLASH FLOODS

### Definitions and Causes

Flash flood is a flood caused by heavy or excessive rainfall in a short period of time, generally less than 6 hours, characterized by raging torrents that can occur within minutes or a few hours of excessive rainfall (https://www.weather.gov/phi/FlashFloodingDefinition).

Flash flood causes include extremely heavy rainfall from thunderstorms, rain-on-snow, rapid snow-melt, dam failure, and levee failure. Multiple factors affect flash flood occurrence including rainfall intensity, location and distribution of rainfall, land use, topography, vegetation types and density, soil type, and soil water content (https://www.weather.gov/mrx/flood_and_flash).

### Global Impact Statistics

In 2020, flood disasters were the most frequent weather event, occurring 43% more than historic levels and causing 6,171 deaths (https://gitnux.org/flood-statistics/). In 2022, there were around 200 flood events reported worldwide, with the Americas accounting for more than half of occurrences and Asia following with 96 reported flood events (https://www-statista-com.libproxy.pcc.edu/study/140946/floods-worldwide/).

Floods displace 32 million people worldwide in a single year. Between 2000 and 2019, floods accounted for 44% of all natural disasters globally, impacting 1.6 billion people (https://statista.com/study/140946/floods-worldwide). Annual global losses from flooding average 40 billion dollars (https://statista.com/study/140946/floods-worldwide).

Research using the Mann Kendall Trend Test on data from 1960 to 2022 determined that the flood frequency tendency is statistically significant, suggesting an increasing trend in flood occurrences over recent decades (https://gitnux.org/flood-statistics/).

### Climate Change Projections

Climate change is likely to make extreme droughts and flooding more frequent. At a 1.5°C temperature rise, heavy precipitation that would have been a once in a 10-year rainfall event will occur 1.5 times every decade and be over 10% wetter (https://businessgreen.com/news/2126092/ipcc-climate-change-worsens-extreme-weather).

More than 1.8 billion people worldwide live in areas with a significant risk of flooding due to climate change and human interventions (https://gazettengr.com/climate-change-put-1-8-billion-people-worldwide-at-risk-of-flooding-report/). In 2024, there were 142 disasters caused by flooding, making it the second most common trigger of disasters after storms (https://gazettengr.com/climate-change-put-1-8-billion-people-worldwide-at-risk-of-flooding-report/).

In snowy, high-elevation parts of the Northern Hemisphere, rainfall extremes increased by an average of 15% per 1°C of warming according to a 2022 study published in Nature (https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/flood-risk-and-climate-change-global-and-regional-perspectives/).

### Regional Projections

Flood risk projections for Tokyo show that areas inundated to depths of 25 cm or more may increase by 1.25 times for 2016-2035 and by 1.55 times for 2076-2095 compared to current conditions (https://doaj.org/article/120334ee66d346619e4ce9586177f4ff). Europe could face water restrictions and a bigger economic impact from flooding due to climate change, according to the IPCC report (https://www.carbonbrief.org/how-much-flooding-is-in-the-uks-future-a-look-at-the-ipcc-report).

### Mitigation Strategies

Structural flood mitigation methods include dams and reservoirs for regulating river flow, levees and floodwalls as barriers preventing floodwaters, and channel modification through dredging, straightening meanders, and widening channels (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flood_management).

Non-structural flood mitigation methods include prevention, prediction enabling flood warnings and evacuation, zoning regulations, physical control through nature-based solutions like marshes and floodplains, and flood insurance policies (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flood_management). Best practice within landscape engineering is increasingly to rely on soft infrastructure and natural systems such as marshes and flood plains for handling increases in water (https://tutorchase.com/notes/ib/geography/a-2-4-flood-mitigation-strategies).

### Historical Major Flood Events

The Burchardi Flood struck the North Sea coast on October 11-12, 1634, overrunning dikes and causing 8,000 to 15,000 deaths (https://www.history.com/news/worlds-most-catastrophic-floods-in-photos). The Johnstown Flood occurred on May 31, 1889, in Johnstown, Pennsylvania, caused by dam failure and killed 2,200 people (https://www.weather.gov/lmk/toptenfloodevents).

In June 1965, a flood on the South Platte River in Colorado caused almost $2.6 billion in damages (in 2007 dollars) in the Denver metro area, making it the most damaging flood in Colorado (https://www.weather.gov/lmk/toptenfloodevents). In the summer of 1931, heavy snowmelt, torrential rains, and seven cyclonic storms produced the most devastating flood in Chinese history, with the Yangtze, Yellow, and Huai Rivers bursting through their dikes and flooding an area larger than England (https://www.history.com/articles/worlds-most-catastrophic-floods-in-photos).

The Machchu-2 dam failure in 1979 in India is considered the worst flash flood in history, with estimated deaths ranging from 1,800 to 25,000 people (https://www.geeksforgeeks.org/?p=1183926). Hurricane Katrina flooded 80 percent of New Orleans, resulting in up to 1,170 deaths (https://www.floodmanagement.info/what-are-the-negative-social-impacts-of-flooding/).

Kerala's 2024 floods and landslides resulted in over 254 fatalities with widespread displacement and destruction of homes (https://www.floodmanagement.info/what-are-the-negative-social-impacts-of-flooding/).

### Early Warning Systems

As of 2021, over 3 billion people in 67 countries are being provided early warnings of potential flash flooding through their National Meteorological and Hydrological Services (NMHSs) working through the Flash Flood Guidance System (FFGS) (https://www.meted.ucar.edu/communities/hazwarnsys/haz_fflood.php).

NOAA, USAID Bureau for Humanitarian Assistance, and the Hydrologic Research Center (HRC) formed a partnership in 2007 to develop and implement the Flash Flood Guidance System (FFGS) for global application (https://www.meted.ucar.edu/communities/hazwarnsys/ffewsrg/FF_EWS.frontmatter.pdf).

Flash Flood Early Warning Decision Support Systems integrate meteorological forecasting, hydrological modeling, real-time monitoring, and communication technologies to provide timely alerts to vulnerable communities (https://www.meted.ucar.edu/communities/hazwarnsys/ffewsrg/FF_EWS.Chap.1.pdf).

### Impacts and Displacement

Immediate impacts of flooding include loss of human life, damage to property, destruction of crops, loss of livestock, non-functioning of infrastructure facilities, and deterioration of health condition owing to waterborne diseases (https://www.floodmanagement.info/?p=2459).

Frequent flooding resulting in loss of livelihoods and production can trigger mass migration or population displacement, with thousands displaced long-term due to complete destruction of homes and ongoing safety concerns (https://www.floodmanagement.info/?p=2459). Flash floods, with little or no warning time, cause more deaths than slow-rising riverine floods (https://www.floodmanagement.info/what-are-the-negative-social-impacts-of-flooding/).

As communication links and infrastructure such as power plants, roads and bridges are damaged during flooding, economic activities come to a standstill, resulting in dislocation and dysfunction of normal life for periods much beyond the duration of the flooding (https://www.floodmanagement.info/?p=2459).

### Future Economic Projections

U.S. flood losses are predicted to increase to $40.6 billion by 2050, an increase of 26.4% from current levels, with Jackson, Mississippi predicted to see losses increase by 49% while Cook County, Minnesota will see only a 7.4% increase (https://riskandinsurance.com/u-s-flooding-losses-could-see-a-26-uptick-by-2050-you-can-thank-climate-change).

Near-term global flood projections to 2050 show smaller increases in flood hazards, with a 7% rise under the low-emissions scenario and 15% under the high-emissions scenario (https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/uk/news/catastrophe/new-research-examines-global-flood-risk-amid-climate-change-502308.aspx). By 2050, low-emission scenarios show flood increases ranging between 7% decrease and 26% increase, while high-emission scenarios show increases ranging between 5% decrease and 43% increase (https://informedinfrastructure.com/post/thoughts-from-engineers-where-the-water-will-go).

Hurricanes will continue to intensify coastal surges affecting Virginia, the Carolinas and Florida, with inland areas also being affected particularly in the Northeast, with more-intense rainfall projected (https://informedinfrastructure.com/post/thoughts-from-engineers-where-the-water-will-go).

Coastal flood risks could potentially increase by 99% by 2100 even under the optimistic low-emissions scenario due to the delayed response of the oceans to existing warming (https://riskandinsurance.com/u-s-flooding-losses-could-see-a-26-uptick-by-2050-you-can-thank-climate-change).

Much of the climate impact on flooding over the next three decades is already locked into the climate system, though cutting emissions now is crucial to slow the rate of sea level rise and reduce future flood risk (https://riskandinsurance.com/u-s-flooding-losses-could-see-a-26-uptick-by-2050-you-can-thank-climate-change).

## VIII. LANDSLIDES

### Types and Classification

The four main types of landslide movement are falls, topples, spreads, slides, and flows (https://www.bgs.ac.uk/?p=27524). Debris flows are a type of landslide that start as translational slides, with water and rubble mixing as the slide moves downslope, forming a slurry similar in texture to wet concrete (https://snr.unl.edu/data/geologysoils/landslides/landslidetypes.aspx).

Slumps are sliding of material along a curved (rotational) or flat (translational) surface, distinguished by upward-curved slide planes causing rotational movement (https://snr.unl.edu/data/geologysoils/landslides/landslidetypes.aspx).

### Causation

The primary cause of landslides is gravity acting on weakened materials on sloping areas, though most landslides result from multiple factors acting together (https://earthsky.org/human-world/what-causes-landslides/). The propensity for landslide failure generally increases as slope angle increases, with significantly higher levels on steep slopes compared to flat surfaces (https://earthsky.org/human-world/what-causes-landslides/).

Heavy rainfall is a key factor in generating landslides through the generation of pore water pressures and reduction in effective normal stress (https://www.usgs.gov/publications/landslide-triggers-and-types). Landslide triggers include intense rainfall, rapid snowmelt, seismic shaking, volcanic eruption, stream/coastal erosion, and natural dam failure (https://www.usgs.gov/publications/landslide-triggers-and-types).

Destruction of vegetation by droughts, fires, and logging has been associated with increased risk for landslides (https://www.usgs.gov/publications/landslide-triggers-and-types).

### Global Distribution

South Asia suffers the greatest number of fatal landslides globally, followed by Southeast Asia and East Asia, with significant losses also in South America (https://blogs.agu.org/landslideblog/?p=32968). Major landslide hotspots in Asia include the southern edge of the Himalayas, Sri Lanka, Chinese coasts, Sichuan basin, Taiwan, Philippines, and Java in Indonesia (https://blogs.agu.org/landslideblog/?p=32968).

Countries with the most landslides per year include China, Pakistan, Nepal, USA, Japan, Italy, Switzerland, Indonesia, and the Philippines (https://blogs.agu.org/landslideblog/?p=32968).

### Current and Historical Fatalities

In 2025, 663 fatal landslides were recorded globally (excluding earthquake-triggered events), causing 5,091 deaths (https://eos.org/thelandslideblog/global-fatal-landslides-in-2025). In 2024, there were 766 fatal landslides causing 4,933 deaths, which was the highest annual total ever recorded (https://eos.org/thelandslideblog/global-fatal-landslides-in-2025).

From 2004 to 2016, 55,997 people were killed in 4,862 distinct landslide events (https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/death-toll-from-landslides-vastly-underestimated). Between 2004 and 2010, 2,620 fatal landslides killed 32,322 people, which is more than half the total number of people killed by floods annually (https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/death-toll-from-landslides-vastly-underestimated).

Landslide deaths are vastly underestimated because databases tend to classify fatalities as caused by the triggering earthquake or weather event rather than the landslide itself (https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/death-toll-from-landslides-vastly-underestimated).

### Historical Catastrophic Events

The January 10, 1962 Huascarán landslide in Peru involved 13 million cubic metres of rock and ice, killing an estimated 4,000 people (https://blogs.agu.org/landslideblog/?p=41804). The 1920 Haiyuan landslide in China killed approximately 100,000 people (https://blogs.agu.org/landslideblog/?p=41804).

The 1933 Deixi landslide in Sichuan, China killed 6,800 people (https://blogs.agu.org/landslideblog/?p=41804). The March 13, 1961 Kurenivka mudslide in Ukraine killed about 1,500 people (https://blogs.agu.org/landslideblog/?p=41804).

The May 22, 1960 Valdivia Earthquake in Chile (magnitude 9.4), the most powerful seismic event in instrumented history, triggered huge numbers of landslides including the valley-blocking Riñihuazo slide (https://blogs.agu.org/landslideblog/?p=41804). The 1248 Mont Granier landslide in France possibly represents the largest historic landslide in Europe, burying several settlements under 350 million cubic metres of rock debris (https://blogs.agu.org/landslideblog/?p=41804).

### Environmental and Economic Impacts

The main environmental impacts of landslides include loss of human lives, destruction of residential and industrial developments, and damage to agricultural and forest lands (https://nrl.northumbria.ac.uk/id/eprint/6308). Landslides can damage or destroy residential and commercial properties, block transportation routes, damage utilities, and cause significant financial costs (https://nrl.northumbria.ac.uk/id/eprint/6308).

Landslides can modify landforms, disrupt ecosystems, and contribute to soil erosion (https://nrl.northumbria.ac.uk/id/eprint/6308). The estimated annual total global economic cost of landslides is approximately 250 billion USD (https://nrl.northumbria.ac.uk/id/eprint/6308).

Landslides are ranked in fifth place globally both in the number of casualties and material losses among natural disasters (https://nrl.northumbria.ac.uk/id/eprint/6308).

### Mitigation and Prevention

The most widely used approaches to landslide hazard reduction include: restriction of development in landslide-prone areas, codes for excavation and construction, physical measures like drainage and slope modification, and warning systems (https://britannica.com/science/landslide/Landslide-mitigation-and-prevention).

Direct methods of preventing landslides include modifying slope geometry, using chemical agents, installing piles and retaining walls, grouting rock joints, and modifying drainage (https://britannica.com/science/landslide/Landslide-mitigation-and-prevention). Excavating the top of a slope improves stability because the driving force of landslides is generated from the head of the slide (https://britannica.com/science/landslide/Landslide-mitigation-and-prevention).

Seeding a slope through dry seeding or hydroseeding adds stability by anchoring soil in place with plant roots (https://britannica.com/science/landslide/Landslide-mitigation-and-prevention). Properly designed catch ditches and gabion walls are effective in containing rock falls from steep cliffs (https://britannica.com/science/landslide/Landslide-mitigation-and-prevention).

Cable lashing and wire nets are inexpensive and simple ways to protect infrastructure from rock falls (https://britannica.com/science/landslide/Landslide-mitigation-and-prevention).

### Climate Change Effects

Rainfall is the main factor influencing slope stability and causing landslides, with the frequency and intensity of high intensity rainstorms increasing due to climate change (https://www.irpi.cnr.it/en/?p=4619). Climate change is altering the frequency and distribution of landslides through complex mechanisms, mainly through altered precipitation patterns, with little attention on direct effects of warming on ground mechanical response (https://www.irpi.cnr.it/en/?p=4619).

The effects of global warming on landslides remain difficult to quantify and predict due to complex interactions between climate variables and slope stability factors (https://www.irpi.cnr.it/en/?p=4619).

## IX. AVALANCHES

### Types and Characteristics

Avalanche types are differentiated into three main categories: loose snow, slab, and gliding avalanches (https://www.slf.ch/en/avalanches/avalanche-science-and-prevention/avalanche-types/). Slab avalanches have a distinct, broad fracture line and require a bonded layer of snow (the slab) on top of a weak layer, with a slope angle of at least 30 degrees (https://www.slf.ch/en/avalanches/avalanche-science-and-prevention/avalanche-types/).

Powder avalanches are dry avalanches that can reach speeds of 300 km/h and cause tremendous damage (https://www.ortovox.com/en/safety-academy-lab-snow/01-avalanche-basics/avalanche-knowledge). Wet snow avalanches are usually triggered naturally by a big rise in temperature, with meltwater or rainwater penetrating the snowpack and weakening bonds between snow crystals (https://www.slf.ch/en/avalanches/avalanche-science-and-prevention/avalanche-types/).

Slab avalanches are responsible for more than 90% of avalanche fatalities (https://www.slf.ch/en/avalanches/avalanche-science-and-prevention/avalanche-types/).

### Causation

Avalanches are caused by a trifecta of factors: terrain, snowpack, and weather conditions, occurring when the weight of snow exceeds its ability to remain stationary (https://www.almanac.com/what-causes-avalanches-snow-science-warning-signs-climate-impact). Most avalanches occur on slopes between 30 and 45 degrees, though wet avalanches can occur on 20-degree inclines in springtime (https://www.almanac.com/what-causes-avalanches-snow-science-warning-signs-climate-impact).

Avalanche triggers include new snowfall, wind-deposited snow, falling cornices, rapid warming, rain on snow, and human activity like skiing and snowmobiling (https://www.almanac.com/what-causes-avalanches-snow-science-warning-signs-climate-impact).

### Fatality Statistics

Global avalanche-related fatalities have declined from an average of 20 per year (2015-2020) to 14 in 2024-2025 (https://slf.ch/en/avalanches/avalanches-and-avalanche-accidents/long-term-statistics). The annual average number of avalanche fatalities in Switzerland over the entire period from 1936 is 24 (https://slf.ch/en/avalanches/avalanches-and-avalanche-accidents/long-term-statistics).

From 1989 to 1999 in France, there was an average of 20 avalanche accidents per year, 50 swept persons per year, and 25 deaths per year (https://arc.lib.montana.edu/snow-science/item/700). Only slightly more than one in two people who are completely buried by an avalanche (head in snow) survive (https://www.r-bloggers.com/2020/09/u-s-avalanche-fatalities-a-history-and-breakdown-in-r/amp/).

Chances of avalanche survival reduce significantly after just 15 minutes of burial (https://www.r-bloggers.com/2020/09/u-s-avalanche-fatalities-a-history-and-breakdown-in-r/amp/). Slopes between 30 degrees and 45 degrees account for 78% of avalanche incidents, with most occurring in February (https://www.r-bloggers.com/2020/09/u-s-avalanche-fatalities-a-history-and-breakdown-in-r/amp/).

### Historical Deadliest Events

The 1970 Huascarán avalanche in Peru triggered by earthquake is the deadliest avalanche in history, killing an estimated 15,000 to 25,000 people (https://science.howstuffworks.com/nature/natural-disasters/largest-avalanche-in-history.htm). The 1916 White Friday avalanches during World War I in the Alps, triggered by heavy snow and artillery, killed more than 2,000 soldiers (https://science.howstuffworks.com/nature/natural-disasters/largest-avalanche-in-history.htm).

The Winter of Terror (1950-1951) in the Alps saw a cascade of avalanches along the Austro-Swiss alpine border that claimed over 265 human lives within three months (https://science.howstuffworks.com/nature/natural-disasters/largest-avalanche-in-history.htm).

The 1910 Wellington Avalanche in Washington, U.S., sent trains into a gorge, killing 96 people, remaining the deadliest avalanche in U.S. history (https://worldatlas.com/articles/deadliest-avalanches-in-history.html). In 1962, a huge mass of snow broke loose from Mount Huascarán in Peru, wiping out Ranrahirca and Yungay and killing about 4,000 people (https://worldatlas.com/articles/deadliest-avalanches-in-history.html).

The ancient poet Silius Italicus wrote of avalanches that struck Hannibal's invading army in 218 B.C., with Hannibal losing 18,000 men, 2,000 horses, and several elephants crossing the Alps (https://worldatlas.com/articles/deadliest-avalanches-in-history.html).

### Impacts

Avalanches are large masses of snow that can travel at speeds of 80 miles per hour, and when they stop, the snow debris becomes as solid as concrete (https://www.commerce.alaska.gov/web/Portals/4/pub/RiskMAP/RX_Avalanche_Info-Sheet.pdf).

Avalanche fatalities are typically caused by asphyxia, trauma, and hypothermia, with an average of 28 people dying from avalanches in the United States each winter (https://worldatlas.com/articles/what-are-the-effects-of-an-avalanche.html). Avalanches can destroy buildings, block transportation routes, damage utilities and power transmission lines, and cause secondary effects like flash floods (https://worldatlas.com/articles/what-are-the-effects-of-an-avalanche.html).

Snow accumulation from avalanches can destroy crops on farmland, causing significant economic losses (https://worldatlas.com/articles/what-are-the-effects-of-an-avalanche.html).

### Prevention and Control

Avalanche control begins with risk assessment and identifying avalanche terrain through geographic features like vegetation, drainages, and snow distribution (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avalanche_control). Avalanche prevention and mitigation involves three major groups of interventions: active (explosive), passive (structural), and social (awareness) measures (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avalanche_control).

Artificially initiating avalanche falls is a standard widely deployed method of reducing avalanche hazard for protection of highways, railways, and ski slopes (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avalanche_control). Avalanche forecasting is based on atmospheric weather and snowcover data, though operational forecasting still depends largely on empirical experience (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avalanche_control).

### Climate Change Effects

With climate change, the frequency and types of snow avalanches may change, affecting rates of avalanche burial and survival, though predicting specific effects is elusive (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8072472/). Mountain snow cover is affected by climate change with increasing air temperatures, resulting in seasonal snow lines at higher elevations and shorter snow seasons (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8072472/).

Warming air temperatures have favored the wetting of snow and formation of wet snow avalanches, which can now reach subalpine slopes with high damage potential (https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/how-climate-change-may-influence-deadly-avalanches). Rain falling on snow is a common trigger of wet-snow avalanches, with water percolating through the snowpack and weakening its structure through melting and friction reduction (https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/how-climate-change-may-influence-deadly-avalanches).

Global warming will have different effects in different regions, with warm and moist snow in coastal and low elevation areas reacting differently than dry, cold powder in high Rockies (https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/how-climate-change-may-influence-deadly-avalanches).

## X. DROUGHT AND DESERTIFICATION

### Current Global Conditions

In 2025, global annual drought affected approximately 30% of the global land surface, with approximately 1.2% experiencing extreme drought conditions, representing nearly triple the share recorded through the 1990s (https://www.nature.com/articles/s43017-026-00785-z). Approximately one million square kilometers of healthy and productive lands are becoming degraded every year, costing the global economy nearly $880 billion annually (https://www.unccd.int/resources/factsheets/desertification-and-drought-day-2025-factsheet).

Since the beginning of the 21st century, around 40% of global land experienced an increase in both the average number of droughts and in their average intensity between 1950-2000 and 2000-2020 (https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2025/06/global-drought-outlook_28488e98/full-report/towards-a-drier-world_6f10e2e5.html). During the past 5 years (2018–2022), the areas in drought expanded by 74% on average compared with 1981–2017 (https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2025/06/global-drought-outlook_28488e98/full-report/towards-a-drier-world_6f10e2e5.html).

### Definitions and Causes

Desertification is land degradation in arid, semi-arid, and dry sub-humid areas resulting from many factors, including climatic variations and human activities (https://www.ipcc.ch/srccl/chapter/chapter-3/). Rising global temperatures have led to increased rates of evaporation and less soil moisture, causing vegetation to die; climate change exacerbates desertification by altering precipitation patterns and increasing temperatures (https://www.ipcc.ch/srccl/chapter/chapter-3/).

Major human drivers of desertification interacting with climate change include expansion of croplands, unsustainable land management practices, deforestation, overgrazing, over-cultivation of crops, and inappropriate irrigation (https://earth.org/desertification-and-climate-change-whats-the-link/).

### Climate Feedback Mechanisms

Desertification exacerbates climate change through mechanisms such as changes in vegetation cover, sand and dust aerosols, and greenhouse gas fluxes (https://www.weforum.org/stories/2019/08/explainer-desertification-and-the-role-of-climate-change/).

### Geographic Distribution

Africa and Asia are the most affected areas by desertification globally, with the highest numbers of people affected in South and East Asia, the Sahara region, North Africa, and the Middle East (https://www.worldatlas.com/articles/which-places-in-the-world-are-most-affected-by-desertification-due-to-global-warming.html).

The Sahel, a semi-arid savannah home to 400 million people stretching from Senegal, Niger, Mali, Chad to Sudan, has more than 80% of its land degraded (https://earth.org/desertification-in-africa/). Mongolia is among the countries most affected by desertification, with 77% of its land already degraded; the Tengger Desert in China is growing at an annual rate of more than 1,350 square miles (https://earth.org/desertification-in-africa/).

Over 30% of the land in the United States is affected by desertification, and a quarter of Latin America and the Caribbean consists of deserts and drylands (https://www.worldatlas.com/articles/which-places-in-the-world-are-most-affected-by-desertification-due-to-global-warming.html).

### Impacts

Desertification causes loss of biodiversity, food insecurity, loss of vegetation cover, loss of forest cover and wood resources, and loss of water reserves (https://www.unccd.int/land-and-life/desertification/overview). Climate-induced desertification has reduced GDP per capita by more than 12% in some African areas (https://www.green.earth/desertification).

Desertification is a major driver of migration as land becomes uninhabitable, compelling people to leave their homes and creating pressure on urban areas (https://www.iberdrola.com/sustainability/desertification).

Socioeconomic impacts of desertification-related droughts include food insecurity, poverty, lowered health, forced migration, and reduced resilience to climate change (https://www.unccd.int/land-and-life/desertification/overview).

### Prevention and Mitigation

Sustainable land management strategies include transhumance (rotational use) of rangelands, stocking rates matched to carrying capacity, and diverse species composition (https://www.greenfacts.org/en/desertification/l-3/6-prevention-desertification.htm). Protection of vegetative cover through planting of trees and shelterbelts has been effectively adopted in developing nations like Bolivia, Mali, Sri Lanka, and Tunisia (https://www.greenfacts.org/en/desertification/l-3/6-prevention-desertification.htm).

Soil conservation measures for desertification prevention include terracing, contour farming, use of cover crops, stopping deforestation, and starting afforestation programs (https://borgenproject.org/stop-desertification/). Improved water management practices include traditional water-harvesting techniques, water storage, and diverse soil and water conservation measures (https://www.greenfacts.org/en/desertification/l-2/6-prevention-desertification.htm).

Local participation and community education on environmental matters and land use innovation, combined with public awareness campaigns, are proven solutions for tackling drought and desertification (https://wrd.unwomen.org/explore/insights/mitigating-impacts-desertification-and-drought).

### Current Regional Crises

Europe is currently experiencing a historic drought, and Central American countries including El Salvador, Panama, and Honduras have declared drought emergencies as of 2026 (https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2025/06/global-drought-outlook_28488e98/full-report/towards-a-drier-world_6f10e2e5.html).

## XI. WILDFIRES

### Overview and Trends

Wildfire is the international term for any unplanned fire in grassland, woodland, heathland or forest, while bushfire is an Australian term for the same phenomenon. The main distinction is regional rather than definitional (https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/forests-and-global-change/articles/10.3389/ffgc.2020.553116/epub).

Over the past 2 decades there has been a 40% increase in the extent of forest fires predominantly in the extratropics, and a 2-fold increase in the intensity of globally extreme fires (https://www.ecmwf.int/node/29500). During the March 2024 to February 2025 fire season, fire-related carbon emissions totaled 2.2 Pg C, 9% above average and the 6th highest on record since 2003, despite below-average global burned area (https://www.ecmwf.int/node/29500).

### Recent Intense Fire Seasons

During March 2024-February 2025, fire carbon emissions were over 4 times above average in Bolivia, 3 times above average in Canada, and approximately 50% above average in Brazil and Venezuela (https://www.ecmwf.int/node/29500).

Models project that extreme fire events on the scale of 2024-2025 will become up to 57% more frequent in Northeast Amazonia, 34% in the Pantanal-Chiquitano, and 50% in the Congo Basin by 2100 under a medium-high scenario (https://www.ecmwf.int/node/29500).

### Climate Change and Wildfire Risk

Climate change increases wildfire risks through rising temperatures, which reduces winter snowpack and produces hotter and drier summers. Snow melting earlier in spring leads to drier soil and forests that stay dry longer (https://www.edf.org/climate/heres-how-climate-change-affects-wildfires). Extreme wildfire risk will increase by an average of 10 days across the continental U.S. by the end of the century, with the southern Great Plains projected to have more than 40 additional days per year of extreme wildfire danger (https://www.dri.edu/climate-change-will-increase-wildfire-risk-and-lengthen-fire-seasons-study-confirms/).

An average annual rise in temperature of one degree Celsius may increase the area burned in a typical year by as much as 600 percent in much of the west (https://www.c2es.org/content/wildfires-and-climate-change/). Half of the increase in western wildfires is due to climate change, and the number of large fires has doubled between 1984 and 2015 in the western United States (https://www.c2es.org/content/wildfires-and-climate-change/).

### Regional Impacts

The Western U.S. has significantly larger wildfires with roughly 20,000 wildfires burning approximately 5.8 million acres in 2022, compared to more than 48,000 fires that burned under 2 million acres in the Eastern U.S. (https://www.northwestsignal.net/news/article_3c5b210b-3309-5f1f-9dbd-999807289088.html).

Forests at high elevations in the Rocky Mountains are burning more now than at any time in the past 2,000 years as a result of extreme heat and drought (https://www.northwestsignal.net/news/article_3c5b210b-3309-5f1f-9dbd-999807289088.html). Seven of California's 10 largest wildfires on record have happened within the past five years, driven by extreme heat and extended drought conditions (https://www.northwestsignal.net/news/article_3c5b210b-3309-5f1f-9dbd-999807289088.html).

Alaska wildfire size and frequency are exceeding historical averages, with wildfires burning more than double the acreage burned during the previous 20-year period between 2000 and 2020 (https://www.northwestsignal.net/news/article_3c5b210b-3309-5f1f-9dbd-999807289088.html).

### 2026 Fire Season Statistics

In 2026, firefighters have responded to 51,774 wildfires that burned more than 8.1 million acres across the United States, which is 126% of the 10-year average for fires and 165% of the 10-year average for acres burned (https://idahonews.com/news/local/new-wildfires-push-2026-us-fire-count-to-51774-burning-81-million-acres).

The Morrill Fire burned 2,603.59 km² (1,005.25 mi²) in 2026, and 2026 Argentina wildfires burned 2,300 km² (890 mi²) (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_fires_of_the_21st_century). 2025 California wildfires burned 2,055.06 km² (793.464 mi²) and resulted in 31 deaths. 2025 South Korea wildfires burned 1,000 km² (386 mi²) with 32 deaths (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_fires_of_the_21st_century).

### Causation

Lightning strikes are the most common natural cause of wildfires, with hot lightning causing more natural wildfires because it lasts longer than cold lightning (https://tempest.earth/?p=19117). Human-caused wildfires account for 87% of all wildfires in the US based on a 10-year average (https://tempest.earth/?p=19117).

Arson, or the intentional setting of fires, accounts for an estimated 20-30% of wildfires in the United States (https://tempest.earth/?p=19117). Common human-caused wildfires include unattended campfires, burning debris on windy days, downed power lines sparking in dry conditions, and arson (https://tempest.earth/?p=19117).

### Prevention and Management

Fire prevention activities take on two kinds of efforts: one to reduce fire brand production and one to reduce the susceptibility of the fuel bed to ignite through fuel treatment or modification (https://ftp.wildfire.gov/public/incident_specific_data/pacific_nw/!SORO/Prevention/XYZ_Archives/Retired_NWCG_Publications/pms455_Wildfire_Prevention_Strategies.pdf).

Controlled burns, also known as prescribed fires, are intentionally set under controlled conditions to remove excess vegetation before it becomes a wildfire hazard (https://greenambassadorchallenge.com/challenge/wildfires/activity/wildfire-prevention). Systematic fire management includes prevention, presuppression (preparedness activities including training, equipping and pre-positioning firefighting resources), and detection (detecting fires early when small) (https://ftp.wildfire.gov/public/incident_specific_data/pacific_nw/!SORO/Prevention/XYZ_Archives/Retired_NWCG_Publications/pms455_Wildfire_Prevention_Strategies.pdf).

Since human activities are responsible for nearly 85% of all wildfires in the United States, prevention efforts must address reducing accidental and negligent ignitions (https://ftp.wildfire.gov/public/incident_specific_data/pacific_nw/!SORO/Prevention/XYZ_Archives/Retired_NWCG_Publications/pms455_Wildfire_Prevention_Strategies.pdf).

### Biodiversity Impacts

Wildfires can both negatively and positively influence biodiversity in certain biomes, depending on the frequency and intensity of fires. Fire is a key process in fire-adapted ecosystems (https://tutorchase.com/answers/ib/ess/how-do-wildfires-influence-biodiversity-in-certain-biomes). In grasslands and certain types of forests, wildfires can help maintain biodiversity by clearing out undergrowth and dead vegetation, allowing new plants to grow and creating more diverse habitats (https://tutorchase.com/answers/ib/ess/how-do-wildfires-influence-biodiversity-in-certain-biomes).

Wildfire releases carbon stored in ecosystems into the atmosphere and increases the risk of soil erosion and nutrient depletion by removing protective vegetation cover (https://tutorchase.com/answers/ib/ess/how-do-wildfires-influence-biodiversity-in-certain-biomes). High-intensity fires can kill large numbers of plants and animals and prevent certain species from recovering, leading to decreased biodiversity when wildfires occur too frequently or with too much intensity (https://tutorchase.com/answers/ib/ess/how-do-wildfires-influence-biodiversity-in-certain-biomes).

The 2019 Australian bushfire season killed an estimated 1 billion animals and pushed some species to the brink of extinction, with 113 species requiring intervention for survival (https://www.publish.csiro.au/wf/fulltext/WF23135).

### Post-Fire Water Impacts

Post-wildfire landscapes are prone to erosion, leading to increased sediment in waterways that can carry nutrients promoting algal blooms, while ash and debris can introduce heavy metals into water supplies (https://www.publish.csiro.au/wf/fulltext/WF23135).

### Modeling and Simulation

Wildfire modeling is concerned with numerical simulation of wildfires to comprehend and predict fire behavior, aiding suppression, increasing safety of firefighters and the public, and minimizing damage (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildfire_modeling). Wildfire modeling attempts to reproduce fire behavior such as how quickly the fire spreads, in which direction, and how much heat it generates, including transitions from surface fires to crown fires (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildfire_modeling).

Extreme fire behavior modeling includes rapid rates of spread, fire whirls, and tall well-developed convection columns (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildfire_modeling). Physics-based modeling of fire behavior enables predictive, physics-informed modeling of wildland fire behavior across complex terrain, vegetation, and atmospheric conditions (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildfire_modeling).

Los Alamos has applied supercomputing resources to capture fire behavior using HIGRAD/FIRETEC coupled software, where HIGRAD is a CFD model which resolves atmospheric turbulent flow and FIRETEC is a fire model which captures fuel consumption and fire spread (https://cdn.lanl.gov/files/wildland-fire-science-la-ur-26-22308_84729.pdf).

### Future Projections

Climate change and land-use change are projected to make wildfires more frequent and intense, with a global increase of extreme fires of up to 14 percent by 2030, 30 percent by 2050, and 50 percent by the end of the century (https://wmo.int/media/news/number-of-wildfires-forecast-rise-50-2100).

A predicted doubling in affected area from 2021 to 2050 has been projected for some regions, with models projecting a 75% to 140% increase in fire activity across Canada by 2100 (https://wmo.int/media/news/number-of-wildfires-forecast-rise-50-2100).

Regions not currently associated with frequently occurring wildfires, such as New England and the Great Lakes, are projected to experience a doubling of occurrence probabilities by 2100 under high emissions scenarios (https://wmo.int/media/news/number-of-wildfires-forecast-rise-50-2100). Fire emissions are projected to increase by approximately 50% from 2001-2010 to 2050-2059 in the western USA, with changes in wildfires and fuel loading contributing nearly 75% and 25% of the total fire emission increase, respectively (https://wmo.int/media/news/number-of-wildfires-forecast-rise-50-2100).

Some of the biggest increases in wildfire occurrence will be in areas not typically used to seeing wildfires, such as the Arctic and central Europe, and areas of tropical forest in Indonesia and the southern Amazon are also likely to see increased burning (https://wmo.int/media/news/number-of-wildfires-forecast-rise-50-2100).

## XII. SINKHOLES

### Definition and Formation

Sinkholes are enclosed depressions formed by the subsurface dissolution of soluble rocks including limestone, dolomite, gypsum, and salt beds, combined with downward displacement of materials (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinkhole). Natural sinkhole formation occurs when rainwater and surface water percolate through soil and fissures in the ground, flowing down to underground soluble bedrock where it dissolves the rock into underground voids and conduits over time (https://www.bgs.ac.uk/discovering-geology/earth-hazards/sinkholes/).

### Types

Sinkhole types include dissolution sinkholes (where depressions form gradually as rock is dissolved), cover-subsidence sinkholes (where soil settles into limestone voids creating depressions), and cover-collapse sinkholes or 'dropouts' (where rapid ground surface collapse occurs) (https://www.jouav.com/blog/sinkholes.html).

### Human Factors

Human activities significantly increase sinkhole occurrence, including mining, coal extraction, fracking, leaking drainage pipes, burst water mains, irrigation, and emptying swimming pools (https://detektia.com/en/sinkhole-causes-risks-prevention/).

### Geographic Distribution

The United States has significant sinkhole activity in Florida, Texas, Kentucky, and Pennsylvania, with Florida, Alabama, and Kentucky being particularly prevalent regions where thousands of sinkholes form annually (https://profession.americangeosciences.org/society/intersections/faq/which-areas-are-most-risk-sinkholes).

Other major sinkhole-prone regions include the Yucatán Peninsula in Mexico (cenotes), Guangxi and Yunnan provinces in China (world's largest cluster), Slovenia, Italy, the Balkans, and the Karapınar region in Turkey (https://sciencenotes.org/sinkhole-facts/).

### Notable Sinkholes

The Xiaozhai Tiankeng in Chongqing, China is Earth's largest natural sinkhole, formed from the collapse of a subterranean river cavern roof, reaching 2,172 feet (662 m) at its deepest point with an upper bowl 1,050 feet (320 m) deep and 1,968 feet (600 m) in diameter (https://www.earthdate.org/episodes/sinkholes).

More than 110 sinkholes formed near Tampa in the Dover area of Florida during a freeze event in January 2010 (https://waypointinspection.com/blog/sinkhole/the-history-and-impact-of-sinkholes/). A massive sinkhole in Shenzhen, China on March 26 killed a construction worker and significantly damaged a residential area (https://sg.news.yahoo.com/china-sinkholes-why-country-host-122728350.html). Another sinkhole near Ningxiang, Hunan province measured 150 meters (492 feet) wide and 50 meters (164 feet) deep and destroyed 20 houses (https://sg.news.yahoo.com/china-sinkholes-why-country-host-122728350.html).

### Impacts

Sinkholes cause cracks, potholes, and complete road collapse leading to road closures, traffic congestion, and substantial repair costs, while also damaging water supply systems, sewer systems, and communication routes (https://un-spider.org/sinkholes). In urban areas, sinkholes can result in partial and complete structure collapse, high economic losses, evacuation and displacement of residents, and in rare cases loss of human life (https://un-spider.org/sinkholes).

Sinkholes create pathways for surface water to drain rapidly to the water table, potentially allowing pollutants to contaminate groundwater and pose environmental concerns (https://un-spider.org/sinkholes).

### Monitoring Technologies

Interferometric Synthetic Aperture Radar (InSAR) uses satellite radar images to detect changes in the earth's surface and can monitor sinkhole-prone areas using InSAR time series analysis (https://core.ac.uk/works/136384152). High-precision leveling and terrestrial laser scanning are practical monitoring approaches that have been applied at sinkhole sites for measuring ground deformation (https://core.ac.uk/works/136384152).

Sensor-based detection systems continuously monitor geotechnical parameters such as soil moisture, ground vibration, and pressure changes, processing data in real time using advanced analytical methods to detect significant changes in soil conditions (https://0-doi-org.brum.beds.ac.uk/10.3390/su11154007). Emerging detection technologies include optical fibre sensing for sinkhole detection and monitoring, and combined seismic and acoustic emission/microseismic (AE/MS) techniques that provide feasible methods for sinkhole detection and stability monitoring (https://abstracts.societyforscience.org/Home/PrintPdf/17081).

Integrated Internet of Things (IoT) and Artificial Intelligence/Machine Learning (ML) approaches using structural health monitoring systems (SHMS) and wireless sensor networks (WSN) can provide real-time diagnosis of underground structural state (https://0-doi-org.brum.beds.ac.uk/10.3390/su11154007).

### Prevention and Mitigation

Preventive planning is the safest mitigation strategy, typically implemented through regulations that exclude sinkholes and their vicinity from development, requiring identification of all existing sinkholes and precise mapping of sinkhole edges and underlying subsidence structures (https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/kip_articles/2003).

Construction of comprehensive and accurate cartographic sinkhole inventories is essential for risk mitigation, allowing identification of subsidence-affected areas and delineation of hazardous sectors (https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/kip_articles/2003).

Engineering mitigation measures include the use of geogrids, mortar injections, and polyurethane injections to stabilize ground and prevent sinkhole formation (https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/kip_articles/2003).

Monitoring and detection-based mitigation measures include high-resolution satellite imagery, radar, and groundwater monitoring to detect early signs of abnormalities, with radar used to monitor groundwater level fluctuations to predict sinkhole formation in advance (https://motivewith.com/en/get-motivated/understanding-sinkholes).

Infrastructure maintenance including consistent inspection and enhancement of maintenance policies for roads, bridges, underground pipes, and sewage systems minimizes sinkhole risk (https://motivewith.com/en/get-motivated/understanding-sinkholes).

Strict regulations on underground construction and development activities, along with strengthened criteria for assessing underground space stability, are essential prevention measures (https://motivewith.com/en/get-motivated/understanding-sinkholes).

### Scientific Advances

Recent scientific findings show that horizontal surface deformations can precede sinkhole formation well in advance, and regular radar measurements collected from above can detect some sinkholes before collapse occurs (https://www.universetoday.com/articles/giant-growing-louisiana-sinkhole-showed-up-in-radar-before-collapse-nasa).

Advanced research methods utilize high-resolution elevation data, numerical simulations, and remote sensing techniques to understand the interplay between geologic, hydrological, and human-induced forces to enhance sinkhole occurrence prediction (https://www.nature.com/research-intelligence/nri-topic-summaries/sinkhole-formation-and-karst-geomorphology-micro-169490).

Innovative methodological approaches integrating morphometric-cluster assessments with deformation monitoring have successfully identified early subsidence signals in regions with underlying evaporite sequences (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8834019/).

## XIII. CROSS-CUTTING PATTERNS AND INSIGHTS

### Climate Change as Multiplier Effect

The research reveals a consistent pattern: climate change is intensifying virtually all geographical hazards. Rising temperatures increase atmospheric moisture, leading to more intense precipitation and flooding. Heat-driven droughts increase wildfire susceptibility and desertification. Ocean warming fuels more powerful tropical cyclones. Changing precipitation patterns trigger more landslides. Warmer air causes earlier snow melt and wet-snow avalanches. This interconnected system means mitigation efforts must be comprehensive and multi-sectoral.

### Vulnerable Populations

Developing nations and regions with lower adaptive capacity bear disproportionate losses. The Bay of Bengal region accounts for seven of the ten deadliest tropical cyclones in history. South Asia experiences the greatest number of fatal landslides globally. Small island nations like Tonga suffer catastrophic economic losses relative to GDP. This pattern highlights the fundamental inequity in how natural hazards affect human populations.

### Economic Costs Escalating Faster Than Populations

Economic losses from natural disasters are outpacing population growth and GDP growth in many regions. Property development in hazard zones, increased infrastructure density, and concentrated wealth in vulnerable coastal areas all amplify potential losses. The $1.2 billion potential loss to Los Angeles-Long Beach ports from a single tsunami event illustrates this concentration risk.

### Early Warning Systems as Critical Infrastructure

Successful examples from Japan, Indonesia, and the Pacific region demonstrate that comprehensive early warning systems combining detection networks, rapid communication, and community preparedness can dramatically reduce casualties. Yet vast regions remain inadequately equipped with such systems, representing a critical gap in disaster risk reduction.

### Nature-Based Solutions Emerging as Preferred Approach

From flood management (marshes and floodplains) to landslide mitigation (vegetation stabilization) to avalanche management (forest cover), the research reflects growing recognition that working with natural processes often proves more sustainable than hard engineering approaches. This represents a fundamental shift in hazard mitigation philosophy.

### Data Recording Gaps Create Knowledge Deficits

Landslide deaths are vastly underestimated because casualties are often attributed to triggering earthquakes or storms rather than the landslide itself. This suggests true costs of multiple hazards are obscured by classification systems, complicating risk assessment and resource allocation.

### Compound and Cascading Events

The research demonstrates that single events often trigger cascades: earthquakes cause tsunamis and landslides; volcanic eruptions generate tsunamis; landslides can create floods; wildfires followed by heavy rains produce debris flows; droughts enable wildfires. Emergency management frameworks must account for these compound effects rather than treating hazards in isolation.

## XIV. CONCLUSIONS

Dangerous geographical events represent escalating threats to global populations and economies. The 1.6 billion people impacted by natural disasters between 2000-2019 reflects the pervasive nature of these hazards, while the concentration of impacts in developing regions reveals persistent vulnerability inequities.

Climate change emerges as the dominant driver of intensification, operating through multiple mechanisms: increased atmospheric moisture, ocean warming, altered precipitation patterns, rising temperatures, and shifting seasonal dynamics. The projections are sobering—extreme fires 50% more frequent by 2100, floods increasing 7-43% by 2050, tropical cyclone intensity rising, and new regions (Arctic, central Europe, Great Lakes) facing previously uncommon hazards.

Technological advances in detection (seismic networks, InSAR, distributed acoustic sensing), modeling (machine learning, supercomputing), and forecasting offer hope for improved preparedness. Early warning systems have proven effective when comprehensively deployed. Nature-based solutions increasingly complement engineering approaches. However, implementation gaps remain vast, particularly in developing nations facing the highest risks.

The fundamental challenge is systemic: hazards are becoming more frequent and intense while vulnerable populations continue expanding into hazard zones, driven by poverty, lack of alternatives, and inadequate land-use planning. Addressing dangerous geographical events requires integrated strategies spanning climate mitigation, early warning system development, building code enforcement, land-use regulation, ecosystem restoration, and disaster risk financing—with priority given to protecting the world's most vulnerable populations.
